# Stop invoicing clients for your poor choice of tools

I’ve been building websites for companies for many years now, and I’ve grown tired of a pattern I keep seeing among web agencies. It’s about WordPress – a tool that has dominated the industry for over a decade.

Here’s how it usually plays out: the agency builds a WordPress site, delivers it to the client, and then starts invoicing them on an ongoing basis just to keep the WordPress core and its plugins up to date. On the surface, it might sound reasonable. But let’s think about it: is it really professional to make the client pay for the fact that we chose a tool that requires constant manual maintenance just to stay alive?

## WordPress gets you started quickly – but it’s immediate debt

The reason WordPress is chosen again and again isn’t because it’s the best tool for the developer or the client. It’s chosen because it gets you going very, very quickly. In just a few hours you can launch a site and the client is initially happy.

But that speed comes at the cost of immediate technical debt. You’re kicking the can down the road. Ask most developers and I’m convinced they’d admit they’d rather be working with a modern stack: a headless CMS like Sanity, Prismic or Contentful, combined with a frontend framework they actually enjoy – such as Astro, SvelteKit, or Next. That way, both the developer and the client end up with something robust, modern, and future-proof.

## Clients don’t care about versions – they care about results

No client cares which version of Ninja Forms, ACF or Yoast SEO is running. What they care about is that their site works, full stop. They expect the system they pay for to simply do its job. The fact that we’re in the background fiddling with updates that could potentially crash the site – that’s our problem, not theirs.

## Technical debt is our responsibility – never the client’s

Charging a client for WordPress updates is in the same category as a development team asking a product owner or senior manager for “permission” to spend time fixing technical debt. Technical debt is our duty to manage – not a burden to dump on the client or leadership. A client should never have to make a decision about whether their site is safe and updated. Just like a manager should never have to approve codebase clean-up. This is our responsibility as professionals.

## Automate what can be automated

The tools to automate this already exist. Machines are excellent at testing, updating, and rolling back changes. Charging four consulting hours to manually click through WordPress updates isn’t value creation – it’s waste. If you want long-term client relationships, deliver real value. Automate what can be automated. Charge for what you actually bring to the table – strategy, design, optimisation, business value – not admin clicks in a WordPress dashboard.

## What we should be doing instead

Do you really want to be the person who bills for manually testing something a computer can do better than you? Or do you want to build a business that’s sustainable, where clients trust you because you take responsibility for your tools and your stack?

For me the choice is obvious: stop invoicing clients for your poor choice of tools.
